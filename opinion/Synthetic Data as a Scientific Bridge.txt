Synthetic Data as a Scientific Bridge: Defending the Proactive Use of Structured Synthetic Datasets
Topic: Neuropsychological Informatics & Research Methods

Opinion by Yll Agimi, PhD, MPHS, MS

In an era of growing demand for data-driven research, one of the most persistent and underappreciated bottlenecks in applied science is not the quality of analytical methods, it's waiting for the data. Researchers embedded in government, military, and clinical contexts routinely face a paradox: projects are funded, teams are assembled, and analytical frameworks are designed months, sometimes years, before a single row of real data can be accessed. For instruments governed by proprietary licensing, protected health information regulations, Department of Defense (DoD) data-sharing agreements, or international transfer protocols, this delay is not exceptional, it's really the norm. Ask any researcher or Government contractor. The Automated Neuropsychological Assessment Metrics (ANAM4), a widely deployed computerized cognitive battery used across the U.S. military and VA health systems, is one such example. Constructing a clinically and statistically valid synthetic version of its output data is not a workaround or an approximation, we feel it's a methodological investment that accelerates science responsibly.

The Problem with Waiting
Government-managed and proprietary datasets impose access timelines that are structurally at odds with the iterative, exploratory rhythm of quantitative research. Data use agreements with the Defense Health Agency or the VA can require six to eighteen months of review before any data transfer occurs (Hume et al., 2019). Meanwhile, researchers must design preprocessing pipelines, select statistical models, write analysis code, and establish quality assurance protocols, all in the absence of real cases. The alternative, deferring all analytical development until data arrives, wastes time, introduces last-minute errors into pre-registered analyses, and strains project timelines. Synthetic data, when constructed from published normative parameters and validated against documented distributional properties, eliminates this bottleneck without compromising scientific integrity.

What We Built and Why It Works
Our synthetic ANAM4 dataset was constructed using a parametric generation framework grounded entirely in published normative literature. The primary sources were Meyers (2020), who established military population norms for the current ANAM4 battery, and Roebuck-Spencer et al. (2017), whose large-scale civilian normative study provided stratified reference values across sex and age groups for all major subtests. Rather than sampling from arbitrary distributions, each synthetic subject's cognitive performance was drawn from subtest-specific, age × sex stratified norm tables, covering six age bands (17–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60+) and both male and female cohorts across all ten primary ANAM modules, including Simple Reaction Time (SRT), Code Substitution Learning and Delayed (CDS/CDD), Math Processing (MTH), Procedural Reaction Time (PRO), Spatial Processing (SPD), Match to Sample (M2S), Go/No-Go (GNG), and the Sternberg spatial working memory task (ST6).

The critical parameters encoded for each subtest included: population throughput means and standard deviations (the ANAM primary performance metric, expressed as correct responses per minute), mean reaction time distributions in milliseconds with empirically grounded standard deviations, accuracy performance ranges modeled with bounded beta distributions to reflect the ceiling effects characteristic of attention tasks, and signal detection parameters (hit rate and false-alarm rate yielding d′ ≈ 4.0 for GNG) calibrated to published psychophysical norms. A Jensen bias correction to adjust base reaction time upward by approximately 2% was applied to offset the systematic throughput deflation that emerges when between-subject age and sex variance is superimposed on a log-normal reaction time generator, a methodological subtlety often overlooked in naïve synthetic data approaches.

Beyond cognitive metrics, the dataset encodes realistic demographic stratification (military occupational specialty, pay grade, service branch, deployment status, operational rotation), longitudinal session structure (baseline and follow-up with plausible practice and fatigue effects), mood scales (ANAM mood domains including anger, anxiety, fatigue, and vigor anchored to military normative baselines), TBI history questionnaire distributions (approximately 15% with documented prior injury), and sleep and neurobehavioral symptom inventories. The resulting dataset spans 460 variables across 400 subjects with a follow-up wave, structurally mirroring what a DoD data extract would deliver to an approved researcher.

Enabling the Full Research Cycle Before Data Access
The practical yield of this investment was substantial. Before a single access agreement was executed, our team was able to: validate the entire variable schema against the official ANAM data dictionary (achieving 71–91% coverage across subtests); develop and test longitudinal change-score algorithms, including reliable change index calculations for individual service members; build and stress-test a data quality monitoring framework aligned to CDC surveillance guidelines (Romaguera et al., 2000); and design and iterate on a full operationalization mapper connecting research themes to specific column-level variables. Every preprocessing step, every model assumption, and every edge case encountered in the synthetic data becomes a solveble problem before the real data appeared. This is not merely a convenience, it is a form of prospective validity work that strengthens the eventual analysis, and forces the equal distribution of analytical effort over the project spectrum, even if real-world processes would have pushed analyses to project tail end. 

The Case for a Global Parameter Repository
The approach we applied is generalizable, but it requires access to normative parameter tables that are not always collected in one place. The critical insight is that for many standardized instruments, whether cognitive batteries like ANAM, CANTAB, or MoCA; physiological monitoring tools like ACTIGRAPH actigraphy; or structured clinical interviews like the PCL-5, published literature already contains sufficient distributional parameters to construct population-realistic synthetic data without ever touching restricted files. What is missing is aggregation.

We propose that the research community invest in an open, curated repository of synthetic generation parameters: instrument name, population, stratification variables (age, sex, education, diagnosis), distributional family (normal, log-normal, beta, ordinal multinomial), means, standard deviations, covariance structures where available, and the primary citation. A structured, version-controlled repository, modeled on existing open-science infrastructures such as the Open Science Framework or the American Psychological Association's data repository, would allow any team worldwide to generate a structurally valid, distribution-faithful synthetic dataset for a given instrument within hours rather than months. The barrier to entry for international researchers, early-career scientists, and under-resourced institutions who cannot navigate complex U.S. government data-sharing infrastructure would drop substantially.

Such a repository would not replace real data. It would honor it, by ensuring that the analytical methods applied to real data are mature, validated, and purposeful long before access is granted.

Conclusion
Synthetic data is not a compromise. When grounded in published normative science, stratified by the demographic variables known to moderate performance, and engineered to reflect realistic covariance structures, synthetic datasets are a legitimate scientific instrument in their own right. For restricted government data like ANAM4 outputs, where access timelines routinely exceed project planning horizons, proactive synthetic data generation is both methodologically defensible and practically indispensable. The time to build the analytical runway is before the plane lands, not after.
